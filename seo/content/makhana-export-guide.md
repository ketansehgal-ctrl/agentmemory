# Makhana Export Guide: How to Buy Foxnuts from India

Makhana has moved from Indian kitchens to health-food shelves around the world. Light, crunchy and plant-based, it's now sold as a roasted snack, a cooking ingredient and a flavoured snack in many markets.

If you're an importer, distributor or snack brand looking to source makhana from India, this guide explains what it is, where it comes from, how it's graded, and what to check before you order.

## What is makhana?

Makhana is the popped seed of *Euryale ferox*, a water plant often called the prickly water lily or gorgon plant. In English it's usually sold as **foxnut** or **fox nut**, and sometimes as "lotus seed" — though true lotus seed comes from a different plant.

The seeds grow in ponds and wetlands. After harvest they're cleaned, dried, roasted and then popped, traditionally by hand. Popping turns the hard black seed into the white, airy puff that buyers know.

Makhana is generally described as low in fat, and it's naturally gluten-free and vegan. If you plan to make nutrition or health claims on packs, base them on lab testing and the labelling rules of your market.

## Where makhana is grown in India

India is widely regarded as the main commercial source of makhana. Most of it comes from **Bihar**, especially the Mithila region in the north of the state. **Mithila Makhana** has a Geographical Indication (GI) tag, which recognises the region's tradition.

## How makhana is graded

There isn't a single global grading standard. Exporters and buyers usually agree specifications based on a few factors:

- **Size (pop diameter):** larger pops usually command higher prices. Indian traders often describe size in millimetres or a traditional local measure called *suta*. Ask your supplier to state sizes in millimetres so specs are clear.
- **Colour:** whiter, more uniform pops are typically preferred for premium retail.
- **Broken percentage:** the share of broken or split pops in a lot. Lower is better for whole-snack packs; broken pieces can suit flour or cooking use.
- **Moisture:** makhana absorbs moisture from the air and can lose its crunch. Moisture should be low and agreed in the spec.
- **Foreign matter and defects:** husk, black seeds, unpopped seeds and dust should be minimal.

Common commercial categories you may see include large/premium "lawa" pops, standard pops, and mixed or broken grades. Names vary by supplier, so always confirm the measurable specs behind the label.

## Uses and markets

Makhana sells into several different channels:

- **Healthy snacks:** roasted and flavoured makhana (salted, peri-peri, cheese and others) for supermarkets and online stores.
- **Indian and South Asian food:** used in curries, kheer (a sweet pudding) and fasting foods.
- **Ingredient use:** makhana flour or pieces for snack bars, cereals and baked goods.
- **Diaspora retail:** Indian grocery stores in markets with large South Asian communities.

Interest has grown in markets such as North America, the UK, Europe, the Middle East and Australia, driven by plant-based and better-for-you snacking. Demand differs by country, so check your own market before choosing a format.

## FAQ

**Is makhana the same as lotus seed?**
No. Makhana comes from *Euryale ferox*. It's sometimes sold as "lotus seed", but true lotus seed comes from a different plant.

**How should makhana be stored?**
In a cool, dry place in airtight packaging. It absorbs moisture and can go soft if exposed to humid air.

**What size makhana should I buy?**
Larger pops suit premium snack packs; smaller or broken pieces suit cooking, flour and value lines. Agree sizes in millimetres.
